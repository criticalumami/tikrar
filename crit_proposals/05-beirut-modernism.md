# 05: Iconic modernist Beirut, as landmark lanterns

Proposed landmark lanterns, after studying photographs of each building. Each one keeps the building's signature elements and its proportions: storey count, height-to-width ratio and bay rhythm. Ratios were measured from photographs and are good to about ±10 %. The website's "Beirut modernism" section draws all six at one common scale (1 unit = 1 mm fired), so their sizes compare directly.

**Scale.** At the village's 1:75 a 14-storey tower would be 0.6 m tall. Each landmark therefore gets its own scale (1:200–1:400), so it stands 130–230 mm tall, about as high as a village house is wide (180 mm). Every opening still respects the studio's limits: openings ≥ 1.6 mm, bars ≥ 1.6 mm, no more than 40 pins per insert set, and cantilevers ≤ 6 mm unless propped.

**Modularity.** A tower is one floor-band mould cast many times and stacked, with the same printed pin comb reused for every band. A landmark adds two or three new moulds, not twenty.

## The six lanterns

### 1. The Egg (Beirut City Center, from 1965, Joseph Philippe Karam)
An unfinished concrete shell cinema for about 1,000 seats.

- **Seen in photos:** a long, rounded capsule with a flat-ish crown and rounded ends, sitting on a four-level open frame of slabs and square columns. A band of rectangular openings runs along the shell's flank.
- **Proportions:** shell length ≈ 2.3 × shell height. The frame ≈ 0.7 × shell height.
- **Lantern:** 1:250, 190 × 100 × 134 mm.
  - The shell is drain-cast as an upside-down bowl in a one-piece mould. The nine flank slots (8 × 11 mm) are cut at leather-hard through a printed guide.
  - The frame is one slab-and-column tray mould, cast four times and stacked.
  - The tealight sits in the frame, so light comes through the open floors and the shell's slots.

### 2. Électricité du Liban (Mar Mikhaël, competition 1965, built c. 1967–72, CETA: Jacques Aractingi, Pierre Neema, engineer Joseph Nassar)
- **Seen in photos:**
  - a long slab of 14 office floors
  - four strong vertical portal lines dividing the face into three bays
  - a deep cellular sun-breaker grid, 3 × 10 cells per floor
  - a recessed, open ground floor on the portal legs
  - a slim service tower at one end, about a storey taller
- **Proportions:** long-face height ≈ 0.9 × length. The service tower is ≈ 1/8 of the length.
- **Lantern:** 1:300, 220 × 48 × 198 mm.
  - One floor-band mould with a 30-pin insert set, cast 14 times.
  - Cells are 4 × 8.5 mm with 1.7 mm fins.
  - The portal legs are a separate cast frame (6 mm wide), and the service tower is one tall module.

### 3. Gefinor Center (Clemenceau, c. 1969–74, Victor Gruen Associates with Assem Salam; structure Walid Jabri)
- **Seen in photos:** dark-glass towers ribbed with thin vertical fins and closed by blank concrete end walls, beside a lower block of white horizontal bands. Both stand on a podium raised on columns.
- **Proportions:** tower height ≈ 2.2 × width. The lower block ≈ 0.55 × its width.
- **Lantern:** 1:400, 185 × 70 × 196 mm.
  - The fins are 2 mm slots at a 3.6 mm pitch, broken every four floors by a spandrel for strength: 90 pins in three sets.
  - Three moulds: tower, lower block and podium tray.

### 4. Saroulla building (Hamra Street, 1961, Karol Schayer)
Offices, shops and the Saroulla cinema, which reopened in 2005 as Al Madina theatre.

- **Seen in photos:** twelve floors of deep loggias framed by projecting floor slabs and fins, eight per floor, over a glazed cinema floor. A thin entrance canopy rests on round columns.
- **Proportions:** height ≈ 1.45 × width. The ground floor ≈ 1.5 × a typical floor.
- **Lantern:** 1:200, 150 × 75 × 217 mm.
  - Loggia openings are 15.7 × 13.5 mm, in insert sets of 32 pins (four floors each).
  - The slab edges are 2.4 mm relief bands.
  - The canopy rests on two B4 porcelain columns, so it needs no prop.

### 5. Holiday Inn (Minet el-Hosn, 1971–74, André Wogenscky with Maurice Hindié)
- **Seen in photos:**
  - a 26-storey slab with a dense grid of small deep-set windows
  - a blank, windowless service tower rising above it
  - a lit crown floor
  - a low podium wider than the tower
- **Proportions:** slab height ≈ 1.8 × width. The service tower ≈ 1/6 of the width.
- **Lantern:** 1:400, 150 × 50 × 228 mm.
  - Windows are 4.4 × 4.5 mm with 3.7 mm bars.
  - One 26-pin comb covering two floors is printed once and reused for all 13 sets.
  - The service tower is a separate closed module.

### 6. National Museum of Beirut (Museum crossing, built 1930–37, inaugurated 1942, Antoine Nahas and Pierre Leprince-Ringuet)
Egyptian Revival, not modernist: the landmark from before modernism.

- **Seen in photos:**
  - a deep portico of four lotus-capital columns between two massive pylons, under a deep cornice and a raised attic block
  - three tall doors behind the columns
  - lower wings with tall slit windows in groups of three, in recessed panels
  - a broad stepped plinth
- **Proportions:** the portico is ≈ 0.8 × as tall as it is wide and ≈ 1.35 × the wing height. Each wing is ≈ 1/3 of the front.
- **Lantern:** 1:250, 232 × 120 × 70 mm.
  - Three modules: wing, portico, wing.
  - Four B4 columns, cast in a two-part mould with the lotus flare, sit on the plinth.
  - Light comes through the three doors behind the columns and the slit windows.

## What the studio would need (later, not built)

- A **landmark mode**: one building per piece at its own scale, outside the 60 mm module grid.
- A **floor-band generator**: one band mould, cast N times, with a single pin comb reused for every band, and a fit check that each comb stays at 40 pins or fewer.
- A **shell element** for the Egg: a revolved, drain-cast form checked for wall thickness instead of pin sets.
- A **portal / pilotis ground floor** for EDL and Saroulla: porcelain legs instead of walls, checked for span.
