# 05: Iconic modernist Beirut as porcelain lanterns

Beirut's modernist landmarks of the 1950s–70s already work as light-filters: concrete shells, claustra veils, ribbon windows. Each one below has a short history and one all-porcelain way to turn it into a lantern. The website's "Beirut modernism" section has night drawings of each.

**Scale.** At the village's 1:75 scale a 14-storey tower is about 0.6 m tall. Landmarks work better as single pieces at 1:300–1:400, or as one-module motifs inside a 1:75 house.

| Building | Where, when, who | What defines it | As a porcelain lantern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Egg** | Beirut City Center, from 1965, Joseph Philippe Karam | A concrete shell cinema for about 1,000 seats, over shops. Left unfinished in 1975, later used for protests, art and screenings. | A hollow drain-cast shell, which is the most natural slip-cast form, in a two-part mold. Set it on a rectangular podium module, with rounded openings at the base for the candle. |
| **Électricité du Liban** | Mar Mikhaël, competition 1965, built c. 1967–72, CETA (Jacques Aractingi, Pierre Neema, engineer Joseph Nassar; Jean-Noël Conan 1962–66) | 14 storeys on four prestressed-concrete portals with 14 m spans, a public space underneath, and a deep claustra veil on the south face. | The studio's claustra role stacked floor after floor, split into insert sets of ≤ 40 pins. The open ground floor becomes four porcelain portal legs. |
| **Gefinor Center** | Clemenceau, c. 1969–74, Victor Gruen Associates with Assem Salam; structure Walid Jabri | About 55,000 m², an office and shopping complex: a lit commercial podium under office slabs. | Two slab towers on a shared podium, with ribbon windows on every floor and a continuous glowing shop band at the base. |
| **Saroulla building** | Hamra Street, 1961, Karol Schayer | 12 floors over 2 basements: offices, shops and the Saroulla cinema, which reopened in 2005 as Al Madina theatre. | Ribbon floors over a bright cinema base under a thin canopy. A 6 mm cantilever needs no prop. |
| **Holiday Inn** | Minet el-Hosn, 1971–74, André Wogenscky with Maurice Hindié | A tall hotel slab that opened just before the war and became a front line in the Battle of the Hotels. Its shell still stands. | A tall stack of single modules with a dense window grid, a few openings left dark. Best as a single landmark at 1:400. |
| **National Museum of Beirut** *(not modernist)* | Museum crossing, built 1930–37, inaugurated 1942, Antoine Nahas and Pierre Leprince-Ringuet | An Egyptian Revival building with lotus-capital columns that stood on the Green Line through the war. | Symmetrical pavilions either side of a portico of B4 porcelain columns, flared at the top, in front of a lit recess. |

Dates differ between sources, so ranges are given where they do.

## What this suggests for the studio (later, not built)

- A **landmark mode**: one building per piece at its own scale (1:300 or 1:400), outside the 60 mm module grid.
- A **shell element** for the Egg: a revolved drain-cast form with its own two-part mold, checked for wall thickness rather than pin sets.
- A **portal ground floor** (pilotis) for EDL-type towers: porcelain legs instead of walls on the ground floor, checked for span.
