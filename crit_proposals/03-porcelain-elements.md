# 03 · New elements, all in porcelain

Rule: **no non-porcelain accessories.** Every element below is cast from the same slip as the modules, so it shrinks and fires the same way. The manual's railings were wrought iron or cast concrete. Here they become porcelain openwork that glows.

The elements fall into two groups:

- **A. In the module's own mold.** No extra parts; uses the existing insert-set system or relief on the facade plate.
- **B. Separate small porcelain castings,** attached at leather-hard (02, step 7).

Group A comes first: it costs nothing extra in assembly time.

---

## A1. Openwork balustrade (cast in the low-balcony wall)

**Reference:** cast-concrete balustrades of 1925–40, repeating panels with balusters, interlaced, lyre or geometric patterns, between pedestals, under a handrail (PDF pp. 34, 46–47).

**In porcelain:** the low-balcony parapet (14 mm fired, about 1.05 m real) is already a wall with a facade. A **balustrade opening type** is a row of narrow vertical openings:

```
  ┌────────────────────────────┐ ← handrail band 2.5 mm
  │ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ ▯ │ ← balusters: openings 1.6–2 mm wide, bars 1.6–2 mm
  └────────────────────────────┘ ← plinth 2.5 mm
```

- Patterns: plain bars, pear-shaped balusters (simplified to a waisted slot), lozenges, circles. Keep each pattern a repeat of 8–13 mm, the 60–100 cm panel of the manual.
- **Molding:** one insert set per balustrade face with a comb of pins. No new mold logic, only a new outline in `holesOf()`.
- **Limits:** porcelain bars ≥ 1.6 mm fired, opening ≥ 1.6 mm. Resin pins ≥ 1.4 mm master, with a 1.5° draft. Add a fit-check line "min bar / min pin".
- **Light:** candlelight through a balustrade gives the striped glow of a lit veranda. It is very effective at night.

## A2. Claustra screen

**Reference:** 1950s–60s screens of 20 cm modular cast blocks (circles, crosses, squares) hiding service rooms (p. 53).

**In porcelain:** a facade (or part of one) covered in a grid of identical small openings. At true scale a block is 2.7 mm, too small to cast, so **exaggerate it to 5 mm cells**: about a 3.2 mm opening with 1.8 mm bars.

- Pattern presets: circle grid, cross, square, offset circles.
- It suits "Modern 1945–70" houses: one claustra face per house, e.g. the stair or kitchen side.
- **Molding:** insert set with a pin array. Watch the total pull force: 40+ pins grip hard. Give the plate two drawer pulls, or split it into two sets.
- **Light:** a claustra face lit from inside is probably the most "lantern" thing in the whole catalogue.

## A3. Facade relief (frames, cornices, panels, grooves)

**Reference:** slightly projecting door and window frames (*encadrements*) with decorated lintels (p. 36); crowning cornices and parapets (p. 40); decorative cast panels between structural lines (p. 42); vertical grooves and corner chamfers (p. 14).

**In porcelain:** shallow relief cast directly with the facade.

- Raised relief on porcelain = a recess in the plaster wall piece = a raised feature on the resin case facade plate.
- **Depth** 0.6–1.2 mm fired. **Draft** ≥ 3° on all sides. **No undercuts**: the plaster pulls off the cast straight outwards, and the plaster also pulls straight off the resin plate.
- Slip casting follows the plaster surface, so the wall stays 2.5 mm thick and a bump on the outside appears inside too. That is fine at ≤ 1.2 mm.
- Relief kinds to offer:
  - **frame**: a band of 1–1.5 mm around an opening, with an optional thicker lintel or a small pediment shape
  - **cornice band**: a horizontal band just under the top scrape chamfer (keep 1 mm below the chamfer so scraping doesn't cut it)
  - **string course / sill line**: a horizontal band at sill height across the whole facade; it ties the 3-module front together
  - **panel**: a shallow rectangle with a simple geometric motif between openings
  - **grooves**: vertical recesses
  - **date / initials** cartouche above the door
- **Light:** relief doesn't let light through, but at night the wall's own translucency is uneven (thicker = darker), so frames show as faint dark outlines. That is subtle and nice. Test it on the translucency tile.

## B1. Translucent window panes (the *mhajjar* glow)

**Reference:** translucent, textured *mhajjar* glass in entrance doors and bathroom windows (pp. 85, 87).

**In porcelain:** a very thin porcelain sheet (0.6–1.0 mm fired) fixed **behind** an opening, inside the module. Lit from inside, it glows warm and diffuse, like frosted glass, and hides the tealight.

- **Making the panes:** a flat open-face plaster mold (one per module type, or one generic sheet mold). Pour slip, drain after 1–2 min to get a thin skin, cut panes at leather-hard with a template. Alternatively, a resin-printed shallow plaque mold with a texture (hammered, ribbed, rippled) pressed into it: the texture gives the *mhajjar* look.
- **Attaching:** at leather-hard, slip-join the pane to the inside face around the opening, overlapping 1.5–2 mm all round.
- **Choose the thickness** from the stepped translucency tile (02, step 9).
- Use it for some openings only, e.g. the central bay of every house. A village where some windows glow through a skin and others are open shows more depth.
- **Risk:** a thin pane on a 2.5 mm wall can crack in drying because the two dry at different rates. Dry these modules under a loose cover, and test first.

## B2. Cantilevered balcony slab with consoles

**Reference:** balconies on cast consoles, simple inverted-step profile (p. 37); cantilevered balconies and canopies from 1930, slab thinning toward the edge, often rounded (p. 38).

**In porcelain:**

- **Slab:** 2 mm thick, projecting at most 8–10 mm fired, 15–30 mm long, with an optional low porcelain parapet or A1 openwork on its edge.
- **Consoles:** two small inverted-step brackets, 4–5 mm deep.
- **Making them:** a 2-part plaster "accessory mold" per balcony type, printed and poured like the rest. Or one open-face mold for the slab and a gang mold of 8 consoles.
- **Attaching:** slip-join under a door opening at leather-hard.
- **Firing:** porcelain slumps near maturity, so a cantilever over ~6 mm needs a **porcelain prop** (a little cast column of the same body) under the slab during the firing. The prop is removed afterwards or can stay as a column, which is historically right too (p. 32).

## B3. Bay window (*kiosque*)

**Reference:** projecting rooms on consoles, 2–3 bays divided by square columns, a panel below, windows in the middle, a decorated transom above, narrow side windows (p. 43).

**In porcelain:** a small open-back porcelain box (front + 2 sides, about 10 mm deep, 25–35 mm wide, about one floor high), cast in its own small mold with insert pins for its windows. It is slip-joined over a large opening in the facade, on two consoles (B2). Keep each face one clean opening, with no bars, and the depth at 6 mm so it fires without a prop.

- Light passes from the module through the facade opening into the kiosk and out through its 3 sides. It is very lantern-like.
- It is the most work, so best kept for one or two late-Ottoman central bays per village.

## B4. Columns and pedestals for verandas

**Reference:** round or square shafts 25–30 cm on a guard-rail-height pedestal, with a capital (p. 32).

At scale that is a 3.5–4 mm shaft, castable as a solid rod in a small 2-part mold (gang of 6). Uses: carrying a cantilever balcony (replaces the firing prop of B2), or framing a full-height balcony module. Optional and later.

---

## What not to do

- No metal railings, glass, plastic or wood additions beyond the existing roofs and slabs.
- No relief with undercuts, and no relief deeper than ~1.2 mm in the module mold. Anything bolder becomes a group-B separate piece.
- No unsupported cantilever over ~6 mm in the high fire.
